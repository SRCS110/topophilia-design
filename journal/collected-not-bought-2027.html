Collected, Not Bought: Why 2027 Belongs to the One-of-a-Kind Piece

The rooms people want in 2027 don't come from a catalog. They come from a life: a table found abroad, a chair kept from a grandmother's house, a lamp nobody else owns.

November 22, 2026 · 3 min read

The Collector

There is a moment, walking into a room that has been put together well, when you can't quite tell what is new. The walnut sideboard might be eighty years old. The linen on the sofa might have been woven last spring. The lamp could have come from a market in Lisbon or a barn sale outside Sebastopol. Nothing matches, and everything belongs.

That feeling is the defining note of interiors in 2027.

The end of the matching set

Across the forecasts for the coming year, designers keep returning to the same idea: the showroom room is over. Matched bedroom suites and sofa-and-loveseat pairs are giving way to rooms built from inherited, vintage and new pieces side by side. Nightstands don't have to be twins. Dining chairs can come from three different decades. What holds the room together is a thread: a repeated wood tone, a shared metal finish, a colour that turns up in a rug and again in a cushion.

It's also a relief. Most clients come to us with pieces they love and want to keep. In 2027, those pieces aren't a constraint to design around. They're the starting point.

Vintage, by the numbers

This isn't only a matter of taste. Designers are sourcing vintage at the highest rate in five years, and 1stDibs' designer survey found that 81% of designers now bring pieces from the 1920s through the 1990s into their projects. In the UK, the vintage marketplace Vinterior reports sales to interior designers up 53% year on year.

Part of the reason is practical. New-production furniture has been caught up in tariffs and long lead times, while a well-made antique is already here, already built, and often better made than anything at the same price today. Solid hardwood and hand-cut joinery don't come back into fashion; they simply never left.

New Heritage: antiques without the formality

The look growing out of all this is being called New Heritage. A genuinely old piece, such as a carved chest, a French bergère or a farmhouse table, sits in a room with clean modern proportions and simple fabrics like washed linen or heavy canvas. The restraint is what keeps it fresh. The antique isn't part of a period set; it's the most interesting thing in a calm room.

Expect a few old-fashioned details to return with it: fringe, pleats, skirted chairs and tables, and the softness of pieces that were made to be lived with.

One piece that carries the room

Many designers now build a space around a single great object, such as a burl-wood console, a sculptural chair or an old tapestry, and let everything else step back. It's the opposite of filling a room in one shopping trip. It asks for patience, and it rewards it.

How we source

Topophilia means a love of place, and that's how we think about finding things. Some pieces come from abroad, found on trips and through dealers we trust. Many come from closer to home: estate sales, workshops and makers across Sonoma County and the Bay Area. Each one is chosen for the room it's going into and the person who will live with it.
